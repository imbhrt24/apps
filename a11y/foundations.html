Chapter 1

Who you are designing for

Accessibility is often pictured as design for a small group of blind users. It is far wider. Disability can be permanent, temporary or simply a situation, and all three need the same design.

1 in 6

people worldwide live with a significant disability. That is about 1.3 billion people (World Health Organization).

1 in 12

men have some form of colour blindness, and about 1 in 200 women.

Everyone

is disabled some of the time: a broken wrist, a bright sun, a sleeping baby on one arm.

Chapter 2

Five ways people operate your screen

You design with a mouse on a large monitor. Your users arrive in five different ways, and your design has to work in all of them at once.

Mouse or touch

What you test with. Needs targets big enough to hit, and nothing that only works on hover.

Keyboard only

People with tremor or pain, power users, and everyone using a switch device. They need to reach everything and see where they are.

Screen reader

Software that reads the screen aloud: VoiceOver, TalkBack, NVDA, JAWS. It reads names and structure, not pixels.

Zoom and magnifier

Low vision users at 200% to 400%. They see a small part of the screen at a time, like a phone layout on a laptop.

Voice control

"Click Save changes." They speak the words they can see, so visible labels must match the real names.

Try the whole keyboard vocabulary

Most interfaces are operated with six keys. Press each one now. Each card lights up when you do.

The takeaway: before you hand off any screen, put the mouse away and use it with these six keys. It is the fastest accessibility test there is.

Chapter 3

What a screen reader actually says

This is the idea every later page depends on. A screen reader cannot see your layout. For each control it reads three facts, and all three are your design decisions.

Name

What is it called? "Save changes", "Delete invoice", "Dark mode".

Role

What kind of thing is it? Button, link, switch, checkbox, dialog, table.

State

What condition is it in? On, off, pressed, expanded, selected, loading.

The same rule for images: alt text

An image has a name too. It is called alt text, and you write it. Describe what the image tells the reader, not what it looks like. If the image is only decoration, mark it as decorative so it is skipped.

Chapter 5

WCAG, in one screen

WCAG (say "wuh-cag") is the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, the standard that laws and company policies point to. The current version is 2.2. It is long, but it rests on four principles.

Perceivable

People can see or hear it. Contrast, text size, alt text, not relying on colour.

Operable

People can use it. Keyboard access, target size, visible focus, enough time.

Understandable

People can make sense of it. Clear labels, predictable behaviour, helpful errors.

Robust

Assistive technology can read it. Every control has a name, a role and a state.

The three levels
LevelWhat it meansWhat to do with it
AThe minimum. Without it, some people cannot use the product at all.Always meet it.
AAThe standard that most laws and companies require.This is your target.
AAAThe highest level. Not always possible for every screen.Use it as a source of good ideas.

Who decides what

Most accessibility problems are created in the design file and only discovered in code. This is the split:

The takeaway: aim for AA, and treat the left column as your job. On every component page the "In your design file" note tells you what to put in the spec.

Nine questions to ask of any component

This is everything above, turned into a habit. The component pages are these nine questions, answered for buttons, toggles, modals and tables.

  1. Can I see it?Contrast for text, icons and borders.
  2. Can I hit it?Target size and spacing.
  3. Can I reach it without a mouse?Tab, Enter, Space, Esc.
  4. Can I see where I am?A visible focus state.
  5. Does it have a name?Icons, images, fields, tables.
  6. Does it say what it is, and its state?Switch, on. Row, expanded.
  7. Does it work without colour?Words and icons carry the meaning.
  8. Does it survive zoom and a small screen?Nothing cut off at 200%.
  9. Does it tell me what happened?Saved, failed, loading, in words.
